What do we actually know about exercise and fibroids?

By Sherri, Accredited Exercise Physiologist · September 2026 · 5 min read

Fibroids are remarkably common - most women will have them by 50, and many never know. But when they do make themselves known - heavy periods, pelvic pressure, bone-deep fatigue - the question that lands in my inbox is always the same: is there anything I can do? Does exercise matter at all?

What the research says

Honesty first: compared to other conditions I write about here, fibroid research is thin. But there is one well-designed study worth knowing. In 2007, a prospective study in the American Journal of Epidemiology followed women over time and found that those with higher physical activity - especially vigorous activity - had a lower risk of developing fibroids. The more active, the lower the risk.

One strong study isn’t settled science, and nothing here says exercise treats fibroids that already exist. What it suggests is gentler: staying active looks protective, and it reliably helps with the things fibroids take - energy, strength, mood.

From my practice

Working with clients who have fibroids is about symptoms driving the plan. Heavy bleeding days might mean a walk instead of a session, and that’s not a failure. Fatigue is real - if your periods are heavy, it’s worth asking your GP to check your iron, because no training plan outruns low iron. On the good weeks, strength work is gold for energy and mood.

The frame I use: we’re not training the fibroid, we’re training you - so you feel stronger and more capable in a body that’s dealing with one.

The takeaway

Early research suggests staying active may lower fibroid risk, and it reliably helps with the fatigue and deconditioning symptoms bring. It works alongside your gynaecologist’s plan, not instead of it.

References

  1. Baird DD, Dunson DB, Hill MC, Cousins D, Schectman JM. Association of physical activity with development of uterine leiomyoma. American Journal of Epidemiology. 2007;165(2):157-163.

This article is general education, not medical advice, and it isn’t a substitute for seeing your GP, specialist or allied health team about your own situation. Everyone’s numbers - and the story behind them - are different.
