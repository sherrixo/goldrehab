How does moving your body actually change blood sugar?

By Sherri, Accredited Exercise Physiologist · September 2026 · 5 min read

If you live with type 2 diabetes - or prediabetes, or a family history that’s put you on notice - you’ve heard "exercise helps with blood sugar". But how? What’s actually happening inside your muscles when you move? Understanding the mechanism makes the habit much easier to keep.

What the research says

The American Diabetes Association’s position statement on exercise lays it out: during aerobic exercise, your working muscles take glucose out of your bloodstream - partly independent of insulin. And after training, your muscles stay more insulin-sensitive for roughly the next 24 to 72 hours. Each session is like opening extra doors for glucose to leave the blood and get used.

Across studies, regular aerobic and resistance training typically lowers HbA1c - your three-month blood sugar average - by about half a percentage point or more. That’s a meaningful shift from a habit, and it’s why the ADA recommends both aerobic and resistance training, with no more than two consecutive days off.

From my practice

Two things I see work again and again. First, the post-meal walk - 10 to 15 minutes after eating is one of the simplest, most effective blood sugar tools there is. Second, strength training: more muscle means more places to store glucose, and clients are always surprised how much difference two sessions a week makes.

One safety note I never skip: if you’re on insulin or certain tablets, exercise can drop blood sugar lower than expected. Loop in your GP or diabetes educator about timing - that’s a five-minute conversation that keeps training safe.

The takeaway

Every session makes your muscles more insulin-sensitive for a day or two. Blood sugar management is built on consistency, not intensity.

References

  1. Colberg SR, Sigal RJ, Yardley JE, et al. Physical activity/exercise and diabetes: a position statement of the American Diabetes Association. Diabetes Care. 2016;39(11):2065-2079.

This article is general education, not medical advice, and it isn’t a substitute for seeing your GP, specialist or allied health team about your own situation. Everyone’s numbers - and the story behind them - are different.
