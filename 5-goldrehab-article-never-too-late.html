Is it ever too late to start exercising?

By Sherri, Accredited Exercise Physiologist · September 2026 · 5 min read

Somewhere along the way, a lot of us absorb the idea that fitness has an expiry date - that if you didn’t build the habit young, the window closed. If you’re 55, 65, 75 and wondering whether starting now is even worth it, this one’s for you.

What the research says

In 2019, a study in JAMA Network Open followed more than 300,000 adults and mapped their exercise habits across their whole lives. The finding I share with clients constantly: people who were inactive in early adulthood but became active in midlife - their 40s, 50s, even 60s - ended up with mortality risks close to people who’d been active all along, and meaningfully lower than people who stayed inactive.

Read that again. The body’s ability to benefit from movement doesn’t expire. It also doesn’t punish you for a late start the way the fitness industry implies.

From my practice

Some of my favourite people to work with are in their 70s and 80s and starting from zero. Their first wins aren’t kilograms or kilometres - they’re getting up from a chair without using their hands, carrying the groceries in one trip, feeling steady on a wet footpath. Strength and balance work, started gently, builds fast when it’s consistent.

The trick with a late start is to start smaller than your enthusiasm. Two gentle sessions a week you can repeat beats one heroic session you can’t walk after. If you have a health condition, a quick chat with your GP or an exercise physiologist will point you at the right starting line.

The takeaway

Starting in midlife or later still delivers most of the benefit. Begin smaller than you think you need to, and let it build.

References

  1. Saint-Maurice PF, Coughlan D, Kelly SP, et al. Association of leisure-time physical activity across the adult life course with all-cause and cause-specific mortality. JAMA Network Open. 2019;2(3):e190355.
  2. World Health Organization. WHO guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behaviour. Geneva: WHO; 2020.

This article is general education, not medical advice, and it isn’t a substitute for seeing your GP, specialist or allied health team about your own situation. Everyone’s numbers - and the story behind them - are different.
