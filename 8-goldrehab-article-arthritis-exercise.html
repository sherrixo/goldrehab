Will exercise wear out arthritic joints - or protect them?

By Sherri, Accredited Exercise Physiologist · September 2026 · 5 min read

If your knees creak, your hips ache, or your hands are stiff in the morning, the instinct is usually to move less - to protect the joint by sparing it. It feels logical. It’s also, for most arthritic joints, exactly backwards. Here’s the evidence.

What the research says

A Cochrane systematic review - the gold standard of evidence reviews - pooled dozens of trials on land-based exercise for knee osteoarthritis. The result: exercise reduced pain and improved physical function and quality of life. Not surgery, not injections. Exercise.

This is why every major osteoarthritis guideline in the world lists exercise as a first-line treatment. Cartilage doesn’t have its own blood supply - it gets its nutrition from movement. Joints, as a rule, like load that’s dosed well.

From my practice

"Motion is lotion" is the phrase my arthritis clients hear most. The biggest barrier isn’t the joint - it’s the fear that pain equals damage. With osteoarthritis, some discomfort during exercise is usually acceptable; the skill is in the dosing. We build strength around the joint patiently, and on flare days we adjust the session rather than abandon it.

Inflammatory arthritis - like rheumatoid - plays by different rules, especially during flares, so that’s a plan to make with your rheumatologist and an exercise physiologist together.

The takeaway

The evidence is clear: the right exercise reduces arthritis pain and improves function. The goal isn’t to protect joints from load - it’s to dose the load well.

References

  1. Fransen M, McConnell S, Harmer AR, et al. Exercise for osteoarthritis of the knee. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. 2015;(1):CD004376.
