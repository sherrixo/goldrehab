What does 150 minutes of cardio a week actually do to your cholesterol?

By Sherri, Accredited Exercise Physiologist · September 2026 · 5 min read

You've probably heard the number: 150 minutes. It turns up in the Australian physical activity guidelines, in WHO recommendations, in every heart-health pamphlet - about 150 minutes of moderate aerobic movement a week. It's one of the most common things clients ask me about, usually right after a blood test. So let's look at what that number actually buys you, specifically when it comes to cholesterol.

What the research actually found

In 2007, a team pooled 25 randomized controlled trials on aerobic exercise and cholesterol - a proper meta-analysis, published in the Archives of Internal Medicine. On average, regular aerobic exercise nudged HDL cholesterol (the "helpful" one) up by about 2.5 mg/dL, or roughly 0.07 mmol/L. That sounds small, but HDL is famously stubborn. Here's the part I love: the benefit kicked in at around 120 minutes of exercise a week - almost exactly where the guidelines landed. And session length was the biggest lever: every extra 10 minutes per session was linked to a further small rise in HDL. How often or how hard people trained mattered less.

A few years earlier, a landmark New England Journal of Medicine study (the STRRIDE trial, 2002) took 111 sedentary, overweight adults with mild cholesterol issues and put them into different exercise programs for about eight months. Even the gentlest group - roughly 19 km of brisk walking a week at a comfortable pace - improved their lipoprotein profile, shifting toward larger, less harmful LDL particles. And this happened without any weight loss. The amount people moved mattered more than the intensity.

What this looks like on the studio floor

This matches what I've seen over 11 years of practice. The people whose cholesterol improves aren't the ones smashing themselves twice a week - they're the ones who find a rhythm they can keep. A brisk walk most days. A swim, a ride, a dance class they actually enjoy. Consistency beats intensity, every time.

Two gentle expectations to set. First, cholesterol moves slowly - think blood tests a few months apart, not a fortnight. Second, "moderate" means different things for different bodies. If you're managing a heart condition, high blood pressure, or anything else in that neighbourhood, it's worth a chat with your GP or an exercise physiologist about what moderate looks like for you.

The takeaway

Regular aerobic movement - around 120 to 150 minutes a week, in sessions you can sustain - modestly but meaningfully improves cholesterol, especially HDL. Going longer beats going harder. And yes, walking counts.
