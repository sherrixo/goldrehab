Can exercise actually help PCOS?

By Sherri, Accredited Exercise Physiologist · September 2026 · 5 min read

If you have PCOS, you’ve probably been given the exercise talk - usually wrapped inside "just lose weight", which is about as helpful as it sounds. So let’s ask the real question: beyond the scale, does moving your body genuinely change what PCOS does? I went to the research to find out.

What the research says

Start with the big one: the 2018 international evidence-based guideline for PCOS - the document specialists around the world actually work from - puts lifestyle change, including exercise, as first-line management for every woman with PCOS. Not as an optional extra. Not only if weight loss is the goal. For everyone.

The same year, a systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 14 studies covering 617 women with PCOS. The honest summary: the data on ovulation were too patchy to analyse statistically, but the pattern pointed toward exercise supporting menstrual regularity. What was measurable was the metabolic side - exercise training improved things like waist circumference, fitness and insulin resistance, with vigorous aerobic work showing the strongest effects on fitness and insulin sensitivity.

From my practice

Women with PCOS often arrive at my studio having been told to train harder and eat less. Here’s what 11 years has taught me: with PCOS, more intensity is not always better. The mix I see work is strength training, low-impact cardio, and real recovery - enough that stress hormones aren’t constantly spiked. Energy and sleep usually shift first. Consistency follows. And that’s when the bigger changes tend to appear.

None of this replaces your GP or specialist’s plan - PCOS is a medical condition with real treatment options. Exercise is the thing you build around that plan, not instead of it.

The takeaway

Exercise isn’t a consolation prize for PCOS - the international guideline calls it first-line. Aim for a mix of strength and aerobic work you can recover from, not punishment you have to survive.

References

  1. Teede ML, Misso ML, Costello MF, et al. Recommendations from the international evidence-based guideline for the assessment and management of polycystic ovary syndrome. Human Reproduction. 2018;33(9):1602-1618.
  2. Benham JL, Yamamoto JM, Friedenreich CM, Rabi DM, Sigal RJ. Role of exercise training in polycystic ovary syndrome: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Clinical Obesity. 2018;8(4):275-284.
